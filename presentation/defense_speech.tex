\section*{Chapter 1: Motivation \& Conceptual Parallels (Slides 5--6)}

\begin{speechbox}{Slide 5: Motivation \& Dual Scope \hfill [02:00 -- 03:15 | Duration: 1m 15s]}
\textit{[Point to the two left blocks, gesture to the UI preview. Do not click the web link yet.]}\\[0.15cm]
``To understand our motivation, we must consider its \textbf{Dual Scope}:\\
On one hand, a \textbf{pedagogical need}: structural dynamics is often taught with static formulas. We aimed to build an intuitive, zero-install web visualizer that enables students to interact with wave propagation live.\\
On the other hand, this raised a severe \textbf{computational challenge}: in parametric design, changing shape in standard FEM requires remeshing, and solving thousands of DOFs cannot maintain 60 FPS in a browser.\\
To resolve this bottleneck, our framework couples \textbf{Isogeometric Analysis (IGA)} for exact geometry with \textbf{Reduced Order Modeling (ROM)} and \textbf{ECSW hyper-reduction}.''
\end{speechbox}

\begin{speechbox}{Slide 6: Conceptual Parallels to Data Compression \hfill [03:15 -- 04:30 | Duration: 1m 15s]}
\textit{[Highlight the Hughes, Quarteroni, and Farhat cards sequentially.]}\\[0.15cm]
``We can draw helpful analogies to data compression:\\
1. \textbf{IGA (Hughes et al., 2005)} is analogous to \textbf{vector graphics versus pixel grids}: CAD splines are retained exactly, avoiding polygonal facet approximations.\\
2. \textbf{ROM (Quarteroni et al.)} acts like \textbf{spectral audio compression}: projecting thousands of degrees of freedom onto dominant energy-carrying spatial modes.\\
3. \textbf{ECSW Hyper-ROM (Farhat et al., 2014)} is like \textbf{optimal sensor placement}: selecting a sparse subset of active cells to compute reduced matrices in microseconds without touching the full mesh.''
\end{speechbox}

\section*{Chapter 2: Theory \& Pullback Mapping (Slides 7--11)}

\begin{speechbox}{Slides 7--11: Boundary Value Problem \& Reference Pullback \hfill [04:30 -- 09:00 | Duration: 4m 30s]}
\textit{[Explain the geometry $\boldsymbol{\mu}=(r, x_c)$, then trace the pullback mapping arrow $\boldsymbol{\Psi}: \hat{\Omega} \to \Omega(\boldsymbol{\mu})$.]}\\[0.15cm]
``We consider a 2D clamped cantilever beam with symmetric notches parameterized by $\boldsymbol{\mu} = (r, x_c)^T$, governing notch depth and axial position. The dynamic behavior obeys balance of linear momentum with Hookean plane-stress elasticity.\\
\textbf{The mathematical obstacle}: changing $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ deforms the physical domain $\Omega(\boldsymbol{\mu})$. Because the function spaces vary with $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, we cannot extract a global reduced basis directly.\\
\textbf{The solution}: we introduce a diffeomorphic pullback mapping $\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}; \boldsymbol{\mu})$ onto a static reference rectangle $\hat{\Omega}$. By mapping derivatives through the transformation Jacobian $\mathbf{F}$ and determinant $J$, geometry variations are completely transferred from the integration boundary into the algebraic coefficients of the differential operators. All solutions now live on a fixed Hilbert space $H^1(\hat{\Omega})$.''
\end{speechbox}

\section*{Chapter 3: Isogeometric Analysis (IGA) (Slides 12--22)}

\begin{speechbox}{Slides 12--22: NURBS Foundations \& Refinement \hfill [09:00 -- 16:00 | Duration: 7m 00s]}
\textit{[Pacing is key here: spend ~35-40s per concept. Emphasize why IGA is uniquely suited to shape parameters.]}\\[0.15cm]
``Classical FEA discretizes CAD models with faceted linear elements, requiring complete remeshing when shape changes. \textbf{IGA} uses the CAD basis functions—B-Splines and NURBS—directly for physical analysis.\\
Basis functions are evaluated recursively via Cox-de Boor. Crucially, moving control points perturbs the geometry smoothly with \textbf{zero change in mesh topology}.\\
For 2D continua, we employ bivariate tensor products. The parent parametric space $[0,1]^2$ serves natively as our reference domain $\hat{\Omega}$.\\
Furthermore, IGA offers \textbf{$k$-refinement}: elevating degree before inserting knots yields high-order basis functions with continuous inter-element derivatives ($C^{p-1}$), eliminating artificial inter-element stress jumps. Following the isoparametric concept, the displacement field $\mathbf{u}_h$ is approximated using these identical basis functions.''
\end{speechbox}

\section*{Chapter 4: Full Order Model (FOM) Simulation (Slides 23--28)}

\begin{speechbox}{Slides 23--28: Dynamic Solver, Stress \& Verification \hfill [16:00 -- 20:30 | Duration: 4m 30s]}
\textit{[Show the dynamic system $M\ddot{u} + K(\mu)u = f$, the smooth Von Mises stress, and the verification plot.]}\\[0.15cm]
``The discretized equations yield an $N = 6{,}564$ DOF system. We integrate in time using the implicit \textbf{Newmark-$\beta$} method ($\beta = 1/4, \gamma = 1/2$), guaranteeing unconditional stability.\\
The computed Von Mises stress concentrates cleanly at the notch roots with smooth continuity across elements.\\
\textbf{Validation}: we proved equivalence between the physical domain solver and the pulled-back reference solver. The numerical difference is on the order of machine precision ($< 10^{-12}$), validating our formulation. An $h$-refinement study confirms optimal theoretical convergence rates $O(h^p)$.''
\end{speechbox}

\section*{Chapter 5: Reduced Order Modeling \& Hyper-Reduction (Slides 29--36)}

\begin{speechbox}{Slides 29--36: POD, ECSW \& Benchmarks \hfill [20:30 -- 26:00 | Duration: 5m 30s]}
\textit{[Highlight the budget $<1.5$ ms, the 28 active cells, the $64.5\times$ speedup table, and present the live demo.]}\\[0.15cm]
``FOM requires 142 ms per step—far too slow for a 60 FPS web application with a $<1.5$ ms physics budget.\\
In the \textbf{Offline stage}, we assemble snapshots across $\mathcal{D}$ and apply SVD. Just $r = 8$ to $12$ POD modes capture $>99.99\%$ of total dynamic energy.\\
However, standard Galerkin projection $\mathbf{K}_r = \boldsymbol{\Phi}^T \mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{\mu}) \boldsymbol{\Phi}$ still requires full-mesh assembly, actually running \textit{slower} online (154 ms).\\
To break this bottleneck, we implement \textbf{ECSW Hyper-reduction}. ECSW selects a minimal sparse subset of just \textbf{28 active elements} out of 3,200. Because ECSW preserves non-negative quadrature weights, symmetry, coercivity, and energy conservation are strictly guaranteed.\\
\textbf{Benchmark Results}: ECSW solves each step in \textbf{2.2 ms}---a \textbf{$64.5\times$ speedup} with only \textbf{0.054\% error}!\\
\textit{[Live Demo]}: Demonstrating this directly in the browser, moving the notch sliders updates the wave dynamics live at 60 FPS entirely client-side without a server.''
\end{speechbox}
