\subsection{Sub-Phase 4.1: Parametric Snapshot Collection and Basis Extraction}
\label{subsec:snapshot_train}

To construct a reduced-order representation capable of real-time parameter exploration, an offline sampling campaign was established across the parametric space $\mathcal{P}$. By varying geometric configurations (such as aspect ratios or hole radii) and external force parameters $\vect{\mu} \in \mathcal{P}$, the full-order state trajectories are assembled into a dense snapshot matrix $\mat{S}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mat{S} = \begin{bmatrix} \vect{u}_1 & \vect{u}_2 & \dots & \vect{u}_N \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times N}
\end{equation}
where $n$ represents the total high-fidelity degrees of freedom, and $N$ represents the total number of sampled design states.

A low-dimensional orthogonal reduced basis $\mat{V}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ ($r \ll n$) is extracted by performing a Singular Value Decomposition (\acrshort{svd}) on the snapshot ensemble:
\begin{equation}
        \mat{S} = \mat{U} \mat{\Sigma} \mat{W}^T
\end{equation}
where $\mat{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $\mat{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ are orthogonal matrices containing the left-singular and right-singular vectors, respectively. The matrix $\mat{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times N}$ contains the singular values $\sigma_i$ along its main diagonal, sorted in descending order:
\begin{equation}
        \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_d \ge 0, \quad d = \min(n, N)
\end{equation}
The truncation size $r$ is determined online by tracking the cumulative energy ratio $\eta(r)$, defined as:
\begin{equation}
        \eta(r) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^d \sigma_i^2}
\end{equation}
The reduced basis $\mat{V}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ is constructed by selecting the first $r$ columns of the left-singular matrix $\mat{U}$:
\begin{equation}
        \mat{V}_r = \begin{bmatrix} \vect{u}_1 & \vect{u}_2 & \dots & \vect{u}_r \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
We choose the minimum size $r$ such that $\eta(r) \ge \theta$, where $\theta$ represents a target energy threshold (e.g., $99.99\%$).

While standard Galerkin projection successfully reduces the state dimension by setting $\vect{u} \approx \mat{V}_r \vect{u}_r$, where $\vect{u}_r \in \mathbb{R}^r$ is the reduced state vector, the governing high-fidelity residual is projected onto the reduced subspace:
\begin{equation}
        \mat{V}_r^T \vect{R}(\mat{V}_r \vect{u}_r) = \vect{0}
\end{equation}
However, evaluating the geometrically nonlinear internal force term $\mat{F}_{\text{int}}(\mat{V}_r \vect{u}_r)$ still requires integration over the full-order mesh at every single Newton iteration. This dependency defines the classical \textit{lifting bottleneck}, where the online evaluation cost remains tied to the full-order mesh size $n$, demanding hyper-reduction techniques.

\subsubsection{Computational Complexity and the Galerkin Projection Bottleneck}
To demonstrate the physical necessity of hyper-reduction, we formally compare the floating-point operations (\acrshort{flops}) scaling per Newton-Raphson iteration of the Full Order Model, the standard Galerkin Reduced-Order Model, and the Hyper-Reduced Reduced-Order Model.

Let $E$ be the total number of physical elements in the mesh, $N_{\text{qp}}$ represent the number of Gauss-Legendre quadrature points per element, $n_{\text{local}}$ represent the degrees of freedom per element, $n$ represent the total number of degrees of freedom in the full-order model, and $r$ represent the dimension of the reduced subspace ($r \ll n$).

The computational costs associated with assembling the residual and tangent stiffness operators scale as follows:
\begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Full Order Model (FOM) Assembly}: Evaluates strain-displacement derivatives and constitutive operations over all elements and quadrature points, scaling as:
        \begin{equation}
                \text{Cost}_{\text{FOM}} \sim \mathcal{O}\left( E \cdot N_{\text{qp}} \cdot n_{\text{local}}^2 \right)
        \end{equation}
        For typical high-fidelity meshes, $E > 10^4$, creating a severe computational burden.
        
        \item \textbf{Standard Galerkin ROM Assembly}: Although the state variable is reduced to size $r$, computing the projected tangent stiffness matrix $\mat{K}_{r,T} = \mat{V}_r^T \mat{K}_T(\mat{V}_r \vect{q}_r) \mat{V}_r$ still requires evaluating $\mat{K}_T$ over the full mesh, followed by two consecutive projection matrix multiplications:
        \begin{equation}
                \text{Cost}_{\text{Galerkin}} \sim \mathcal{O}\left( E \cdot N_{\text{qp}} \cdot n_{\text{local}}^2 + n \cdot r^2 + n \cdot r \right)
        \end{equation}
        Because $E \cdot N_{\text{qp}} \cdot n_{\text{local}}^2$ remains the dominant term, the online cost remains strictly tied to the high-fidelity mesh size $n$. This represents the classical \textit{lifting bottleneck}, rendering the standard Galerkin ROM too slow for client-side execution budgets.
        
        \item \textbf{Hyper-Reduced ROM (ECSW)}: Bypasses the full-order assembly entirely by computing virtual work integrals over a drastically reduced set of active elements $\widehat{\Omega}$ (where $|\widehat{\Omega}| \ll E$):
        \begin{equation}
                \text{Cost}_{\text{ECSW}} \sim \mathcal{O}\left( |\widehat{\Omega}| \cdot N_{\text{qp}} \cdot n_{\text{local}}^2 + r^2 \right)
        \end{equation}
        Since $|\widehat{\Omega}|$ is independent of the full mesh scale, the hyper-reduced engine successfully achieves $\mathcal{O}(1)$ performance scaling relative to the mesh size $n$, enabling real-time execution in the browser.
\end{itemize}
